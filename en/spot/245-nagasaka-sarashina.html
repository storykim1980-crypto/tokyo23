Local Knowledge

Obvious to locals, invisible to visitors

There is a simple reason guidebooks leave this out: to the people who live here it is too obvious to be worth explaining.

汁
How to order
Two dips arrive — alternate, do not mix

Houses in this line serve a sweet dip and a sharp one together. You are not meant to combine them but to switch from one to the other, mouthful by mouthful. The pale gozen soba is light in aroma, so the difference in the dips shows clearly.

三
The backstory
Three shops in this district share one root

Three shops in Azabu-juban descend from the house founded in 1789. Sarashina Horii, this Nagasaka Sarashina Nunoya Tahei, and Azabu Nagasaka Sarashina Honten. Same root, three separate companies.

廃
Heads-up
The original Nunoya Tahei closed in 1941

All three advertise 1789, but the original house wound up in 1941; the three present shops were each re-established after the war. Sources disagree about which is the &#x27;main line&#x27;, so the accurate thing to hold onto is simply that all three branch from the same root.

What it is

A soba house whose line begins, it says, when the first Nunoya Tahei hung out his sign at Azabu Nagasaka in the early Kansei era. It belongs to the sarashina tradition, milling only the core of the buckwheat grain for pale noodles, and serves two dips — one sweet, one sharp.

What to order

Gozen soba
Pure white noodles milled from the heart of the buckwheat grain — the signature of this lineage.
About ¥1,000
Two dipping sauces
Two dips arrive: a sweet one and a sharp one. You alternate between them.

Why locals keep coming

Three shops in Azabu-juban trace themselves to the same 1789 house. Sarashina Horii, this one, and Azabu Nagasaka Sarashina Honten. The root is shared but the companies are separate, and the original Nunoya Tahei itself wound up in 1941.
