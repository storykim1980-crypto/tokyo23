Local Knowledge

Obvious to locals, invisible to visitors

There is a simple reason guidebooks leave this out: to the people who live here it is too obvious to be worth explaining.

年
Local timing
You can climb it on about three occasions a year

It is closed for conservation most of the year. Climbing is permitted only over the first three days of January, on 1 July for the opening of the mountain, and on the second Saturday and Sunday of September during the shrine&#x27;s annual festival. On 1 July the hours are 9am to about 3pm.

講
The backstory
A mountain built in town for those who could not reach the real one

Born of the Fuji devotion that flourished in the Edo period, these mounds were raised so that people who could never make the climb could still make the pilgrimage — the merit was held to be the same. The surface is faced with genuine lava carried from the mountain.

靴
Unwritten rule
Heeled shoes will not do

Eight metres sounds like nothing, but the path is built from stacked natural rock and the footing is uneven. Heels are entirely unsuitable, and after rain the surface turns slippery. Visitors are advised to bring a handkerchief or towel.

参
How to order
Pay respects at the shrine first, then climb

The mound sits directly behind the shrine hall, and the shrine itself is a site of Fuji devotion — so the order is to pay respects first, then climb. Follow the path and mark off the stations as you go, first, second, and on up.

駅
Heads-up
Thirty seconds from the ticket gate

Step out of the north exit at Ekoda and it is right there. A nationally designated folk cultural property standing in front of a station — plenty of people walk past without noticing. A tunnel beside the shrine entrance leads under the tracks to a strip of restaurants and bars.

What it is

A man-made Mount Fuji, raised in the middle of a neighbourhood by Edo townspeople who could not make the real pilgrimage. At roughly eight metres high and thirty across it is among the largest in Tokyo, designated an Important Tangible Folk Cultural Property. Said to date from 1839. It is faced with lava from Fuji itself; a stone shrine crowns the summit, and stone figures of tengu and sacred monkeys stand at the base. Directly outside the north exit of Ekoda Station.

What to order

Why locals keep coming

More than sixty of these mounds survive in Tokyo; only five hold the national Important Tangible Folk Cultural Property designation, and this is one. It is closed for conservation most of the year, opening for climbing only on set dates.
